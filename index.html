Sustainability in project management
Responsibilities, principles, and monitoring
What is sustainability in project management?
An approach to business that balances the environmental, social, and economic aspects of project-based working to meet the current needs of stakeholders without compromising or overburdening future generations.
The key principle
Building sustainability into the vision at project inception is key. If sustainability is planned in as a key requirement from the outset, it cannot then be taken out. It cannot be added retrospectively.
Exam tip: sustainability must be embedded at inception / initiation — not retrofitted at a later stage.
The three core principles
Balance
Address environmental, social, and economic needs without prioritising one at the expense of others.
Integration
Built into governance, processes, and culture — not treated as a separate or secondary track.
Foresight
Anticipate long-term impacts and design solutions that adapt to evolving expectations and regulations.
Sustainability across the lifecycle
Initiation
Include sustainability objectives in the business case and stakeholder analysis.
Planning
Assess resource selection, energy usage, and supply chain impact.
Execution
Decisions on materials, logistics, and workforce welfare.
Closure
How assets are decommissioned or repurposed affects the sustainability footprint.
The four dimensions of sustainability
Sustainability requires balancing all four — they are deeply interrelated and trade-offs between them are common.
Environmental
Climate change, biodiversity, energy efficiency. Reducing ecological footprint — carbon, waste, resource use.
Social
Human rights, equity and equality, community. Fairness, well-being, health & safety, labour practices.
Economic
Affordability, profit, risk management. Fair wages, ethical procurement, lifecycle costs.
Administrative
Legislation & regulation, health & safety, resource efficiency. Governance, policies, and standards.
Trade-offs
Trade-offs between dimensions are common and must be managed transparently. Example: recycled materials may increase costs (economic) but improve environmental outcomes. Use impact assessments, sustainability dashboards, and cost-benefit analysis to manage these transparently.
Sustainability vision and objectives
A sustainability vision defines overarching goals. Objectives translate aspirations into measurable targets. Involves both individual and organisational responsibility. The business case should be aligned to support the sustainability agenda.
Global frameworks to align with
UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
17 global goals providing a shared blueprint for peace and prosperity.
Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG)
Criteria used by investors to evaluate sustainability and ethical impact. Projects aligned with ESG gain investor confidence.
Paris Agreement
International treaty on climate change. High-emission projects must consider alignment with carbon reduction commitments.
What sustainability initiatives influence
  • Product design and manufacturing processes
  • Energy consumption and waste management
  • Procurement policies and supplier selection
  • Stakeholder relationships and community engagement
  • Performance metrics — measured alongside time, cost, and scope
Setting effective sustainability objectives
  • Realistic, measurable, and adaptable
  • Supported by clear KPIs, baselines, and reporting tools
  • Revisited throughout the lifecycle
  • Aligned with business strategy and operational practices
Why monitoring and reporting matter
Without monitoring and reporting, sustainability risks becoming an aspiration rather than an achievement. These mechanisms provide visibility, accountability, and insight.
Establishing baselines
Before any improvements can be measured, the current status must be understood. Baselines should cover: energy usage, waste output, carbon emissions, supplier practices, and employee well-being indicators. Aligned with organisational sustainability standards or external frameworks.
Embedding monitoring in governance
Sustainability metrics incorporated into regular status reports, dashboard reviews, risk logs, and decision points — reviewed by the project board with the same rigour as financial or schedule data.
Sustainability sits alongside time, cost, and quality in governance reporting — not as a peripheral concern.
Quantitative vs qualitative measurement
Quantitative
Emissions reduction, resource usage, compliance rates.
Qualitative
Stakeholder feedback, perception surveys, inclusivity and social impact assessment.
Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)
An international standard for sustainability reporting. Provides frameworks for data collection, data analysis techniques, and continuous improvement processes. Reports should include: progress against targets, explanations for variances, corrective actions, and plans for improvement.
Why stakeholder perception matters
Sustainability is not only about processes and performance — it is also about perception and trust. If stakeholders do not believe a project is genuinely committed to sustainability, they may withhold support, reduce collaboration, or escalate opposition.
Four stakeholder perception areas
Internal communities
Adjust culture internally to make sustainability part of day-to-day thinking. Must be "we want to do it" — not "we are made to do it".
External communities
Report on carbon emissions, energy consumption, waste management, social impact, and ethical practices. Messages should be tailored to different audiences.
Environmental, social & economic impacts
Transparency, credibility, and the importance of data and metrics are key. Aim for sustainable procurement.
Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)
Provides support for data collection, analysis techniques, and continuous improvement processes for sustainability practices.
Key phrases to know
  • "We want to do it" not "we are made to do it" — culture must be intrinsically motivated
  • Sustainability must move from aspiration to active behaviour through performance data
  • Transparent reporting on emissions, waste, and labour practices = proactive stance
  • Tailored communication for different audiences builds credibility

Business case
Five elements, analysis tools, and baseline reviews
Five elements of the business case
1. Strategic context
The compelling case for change.
2. Economic analysis
Return on investment, based on investment appraisal of options.
3. Commercial approach
Derived from the procurement strategy.
4. Financial case
Affordability to the organisation within the time frame.
5. Management approach
Roles, governance structure, life cycle choice, etc.
Exam tip: match each element to its one-line purpose — strategic = why change, economic = return, commercial = procurement, financial = affordability, management = how it will be run.
Analysis tools and techniques
Three tools help examine the environment around a project and the risks and opportunities it faces.
PESTLE
Political, Economic, Sociological, Technical, Legal and Environmental influences that could impact the project.
SWOT
Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats relating to the project.
VUCA
The Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity and Ambiguity relating to the project.
SWOT — internal vs external
Strengths
Strengths of the organisation and their influence / impact on the project.
Weaknesses
Weaknesses of the organisation, their impact on the project, and the risk they might pose.
Opportunities
Elements outside the organisation that the project could exploit.
Threats
External elements outside the organisation that could be problematic, and the risk they might pose.
Exam tip: strengths and weaknesses are internal to the organisation; opportunities and threats are external.
PESTLE — prompt questions
Political
Might lobbying or a change of government impact the project? Local government policies? Office politics?
Economic
Is the project subject to market volatility, exchange rates, inflation? Availability of funding vs financial benefits.
Social
Will the project impact people’s health or living standards? Will it require a change in behaviour or attitudes?
Technological
How mature is the current technology, and will it support the proposed solution? Is technology likely to change?
Legal
Is new legislation likely? Is contract law or employment law a factor? Internal policies which may constrain us?
Environmental
Does the project have an environmental impact? Sustainability issues.
VUCA — factor and response
Each VUCA factor has a matching leadership response that spells out the same letters.
V — Volatility → Vision
Fast, unpredictable, non-repeatable change (e.g. material prices during market turmoil). Accept and embrace change, keep your end goal in mind.
U — Uncertainty → Understanding
Lack of predictability; the past is not an accurate predictor of the future. Pause for thought, invest in analysis of the environment.
C — Complexity → Clarity
Multiple forces, chaos and confusion; hard to know what affects what. One person can’t understand all, so develop teams and communicate well.
A — Ambiguity → Agility
Fuzziness of reality, potential for misreads, cause and effect confusion (who, what, when, where, how, why unclear). Plan ahead in small chunks, be flexible, build in contingency.
Purpose of the business case
It justifies the initial and continued investment in the project, and enables the project manager to agree success criteria with the sponsor and other stakeholders. It also records the decisions made by governance and documents the options considered.
Reviews through the life cycle
  • Reviewed periodically and at decision gates, to ensure the investment remains aligned to expected benefits
  • In the definition and deployment phases, reviewed against progress made, and progress is checked against the benefits outlined
How the project manager uses it
The business case defines why the project is needed, so it is the starting point for developing the project management plan.
When benefits are no longer viable
If the benefits are no longer viable, the senior management team may stop the project.
Exam tip: the business case is a living document — reviewed at gates, not written once and filed away.

Procurement
Strategy, supplier selection, negotiation, and contracts
What is procurement?
  • The process of acquiring goods and services
  • Includes developing a procurement strategy, preparing contracts, selecting and acquiring suppliers, and managing the contract
Purpose of a procurement strategy
Before an organisation, public or private, invests funds, a project procurement strategy must be created to ensure that:
  • Budgets are not exceeded
  • The investment provides the return or benefit originally intended
  • The investment aligns with the bigger-picture strategy of the organisation
To be useful, it must document a process for acquiring goods and/or services and their subsequent management.
What a procurement strategy typically includes
  • Make or buy decision
  • Supplier selection: the selection process; the decision on single, integrated or multiple suppliers; the plan for negotiation
  • Contractual relationship: conditions and forms of contract; supplier reimbursement terms
Exam tip: the sample paper asks for a strategy’s typical contents and for two factors to consider when setting it — know this list.
The procurement strategy
A project document describing the mechanics of how the project will go about procuring and subsequently managing services and goods.
  • Formulated alongside the business case
  • May also be a component of the project management plan, as there are numerous procurement implications for the project manager throughout the life of the project
  • Forms a communication tool between the project manager and project stakeholders
Exam tip: the strategy is formulated alongside the business case, and doubles as a communication tool with stakeholders.
Make or buy decision
Should the goods be made in-house, or could we procure them elsewhere at a lower cost or better quality?
Specification
Understand the functional and technical specification required to understand the cost and time needed.
Capacity, speed and availability
Including the benefits of specialist or technical support.
Quality
Define the required quality aspirations.
Stakeholders
Engage with relevant internal and external stakeholder groups.
Strategy and value for money
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not on the slides.
  • Value for money covers life cycle costs, sustainability, innovation and supplier relationships — not just price
  • The strategy also sets the sourcing model, prequalification criteria, performance metrics, escalation and dispute resolution, and is revised as the project or market changes
  • Make or buy: also weigh intellectual property, regulation and cybersecurity; a hybrid (some in house, some bought) is possible
Supplier selection process
  • Many organisations have a specific process for selecting suppliers, with rules and guidelines to follow
  • There may be guidelines for a competitive tender process: several suppliers are asked to submit prices for a specified piece of work in a commercially competitive manner
  • Objectives: achieve best value for the price charged, and ensure the process and selection are transparent and fair
  • Important that qualified resources are engaged in the process
Tender process
1. Research the market
Define requirements, and review the number of potential suppliers.
2. Pre-qualify suppliers
Check capability, financial stability and references.
3. Issue an Invitation to Tender (ITT)
Instructions, questions, evaluation criteria and scoring matrix.
4. Respond to queries from bidders
Opportunity to seek clarifications.
5. Receive and evaluate the bids
Against selection criteria; consider compliance with the ITT and added value.
6. Award a contract to a successful bidder
Agree the terms of a binding contract, understanding respective liabilities and responsibilities.
7. Enter into contract and contract administration
In accordance with the contract terms agreed.
Exam tip: learn the seven steps in order. Prequalification comes before the ITT; responding to bidder queries comes after it.
Selection extras
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not on the slides.
  • Criteria such as price, quality, capability and delivery are weighted and scored, making decisions traceable and defensible
  • Earlier-stage documents you may meet: PQQ (prequalification questionnaire) and EOI (expression of interest); market engagement may use an RFP, RFQ or ITT
Plan for negotiation
When trying to reach an agreement with a selected supplier, several negotiation techniques could be used.
BATNA — Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement
The best fallback position. Useful to understand this position for both parties.
ZOPA — Zone of Possible Agreement
The bargaining range where the lowest of the supplier’s quoted price range and the highest of the customer’s price range overlap. The negotiation is successful when the final agreement lands anywhere within the ZOPA range.
‘Win Win’
A collaborative approach. Focuses on problem-solving and communication. Depends on a strong trust-based relationship.
ZOPA example from the slide
Software suppliers’ price range
£1m – £1.4m
Project software budget
£900k – £1.2m
ZOPA (the overlap)
£1m to £1.2m
Exam tip: ZOPA is the overlap of the two ranges: it starts at the lowest supplier price and ends at the highest the customer will pay. The sample paper mark scheme words it as the difference between the project maximum and the supplier’s minimum — here £1m to £1.2m.
Contractual relationships
How many suppliers should we buy from? How long-term do we want the relationship to be?
Single contract
Simplest form. Client buys services / goods from one supplier.
Parallel contracts
Duplication or replication of work. Several different contracts where suppliers are doing related work at the same time. The client holds multiple contractual relationships.
Sequential contracts
Dependency between activities or work packages. Completion of one activity is needed before another commences. The client holds multiple contractual relationships.
Prime contracts
Management of sub-contracted work. The client holds one contractual relationship, but sub-contractors deliver elements of the specialist work for the prime contractor.
Exam tip: parallel and sequential = client holds multiple relationships; prime = client holds one.
Pros and cons: single, multiple or integrated suppliers
The supplier strategy chosen affects the cost and time required for procurement and contract negotiation, the leverage on product pricing and the level of responsiveness of the supplier(s).
Single supplier
+ Preferred supplier with strong existing relationship used for all requirements: preferential rates / volume pricing.
+ Less time and cost attached to procurement.
− Risk of over-reliance on one supplier: shortages, disruption.
Multiple suppliers
+ Price competition between suppliers.
+ Reduced risk of supply shortage / disruption.
− Increased cost in contract negotiations and management resource.
Integrated supplier
+ Integration of supplier into the project team.
+ Optimum service delivery through immediacy of contact, easier communications, greater transparency and understanding.
− Difficulties in feeding back issues in service or maintaining confidentiality of certain aspects of the project.
Conditions and forms of contract
A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties setting out the obligations of each party.
What there should be
  • An offer made by one party, and acceptance by the other
  • Intention to create legal relations between the parties
  • Consideration passing from one party to the other in return for the goods or services covered
  • Definite terms, so it is clear what conditions the parties are agreeing
  • Legality — only properly incorporated firms or competent persons entering into contracts
Typical contract conditions
General information; provider’s responsibilities (design and subcontracting); time; quality; payment; dealing with change; compensation or risk; property ownership; insurances; dispute resolution.
Specialist advice
Should be sought by the project team when amending, drafting and agreeing contracts, so risk exposure and legal ramifications are clearly understood.
Bespoke forms
Can be drafted to apply to specific projects or procurement requirements.
Standard forms
In engineering and construction: the Joint Contracts Tribunal (JCT) and the New Engineering Contract (NEC), which have established best practice and are industry recognised.
Relationship notes
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not on the slides.
  • Single contract: easy to manage with one point of accountability, but no contractual fallback if the supplier fails
  • Parallel: competitive tension, but risk of duplication and scope disputes — needs strong interface management
  • Sequential: a delay by one supplier cascades to the next
  • Prime: lower client admin, but less visibility of subcontractor performance and pricing
Supplier reimbursement terms
The approach chosen is often based on the level of risk and how it is apportioned between the parties. It can also depend on the maturity of scope development and understanding of what is required.
Time and materials / unit price
e.g. £100 per hour; buy 10 at £10 each, buy 100 at £9 each.
Client pays per unit. Time overrun risk lies with the client. Suitable when the specification is vague, because it allows the scope to evolve over time.
Cost plus
Agree all reasonable costs will be reimbursed plus an agreed fee.
Client pays actual cost of work plus an agreed amount for supplier overhead and profit. Profit could be a percentage (lower risk for supplier) or a fixed fee (higher risk for supplier). Suitable when an earlier start on site is required and the scope is still being developed — e.g. emergency or urgent works.
Contract target cost / price
Target cost of £20k in the contract, plus agreed fee. If final cost is less than target, the supplier gets a share of the saving.
Target agreed upfront; client and supplier work together to achieve it. Risk is shared at an agreed rate. Incentivised collaboration. Suitable where contractual relationships are collaborative and there are opportunities to incentivise collaborative ideas.
Fixed price
Agree £20k in the contract, pay them £20k.
Price agreed on an agreed scope of work. Time overrun risk lies with the contractor. Price may be higher due to the greater risk for the supplier. Suitable where scope is clearly defined and a transactional relationship is preferred or tolerated.
Other names you may see
  • Fixed price = lump sum
  • Time and materials / unit price = price per unit quantity
  • Target cost = target price
  • Cost plus = cost plus fee
Risk spectrum
Moving from time and materials → cost plus → target cost → fixed price, risk to the client falls and risk to the supplier rises.
Exam tip: time and materials = most risk with the client; fixed price = most risk with the supplier.

Reviews
Review types, reporting, information, and re-planning
What reviews are for
Reviews are one of the principal mechanisms to ensure the quality of deliverables, the performance of the management process and the on-going viability of project work. They gather information to assess the status of the project and its ongoing viability.
Independent perspective
Project reviews provide an independent perspective, ensuring important details aren’t overlooked and that the project is progressing against the original plan and specifications. They also let project managers adapt to fluid requirements, including allocating or re-allocating resources, staff and expertise.
Exam tip: reviews take place throughout the life cycle — learning outcomes name decision gates, benefits reviews and audits.
Why reviews matter
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not in the study pack.
  • Gather evidence-based information on milestones, quality, and whether the business case still holds
  • Accountability and transparency, especially on complex or high-risk projects
  • Record lessons learned and feed organisational learning
  • Each review is a control point: continue, revise or stop
  • Frequency and type should reflect size, complexity and strategic importance
  • Early detection of scope creep, budget overruns or stakeholder dissatisfaction reduces rework and protects the investment
Three types of review
Decision gates
When: Linear — event driven, held at the end of each stage. Iterative — time-bound, held at the end of each iteration.
Benefits: increased senior stakeholder involvement; gain agreement from the project sponsor and governance board; ensure the project is on track and delivering the benefits in the original business case.
Benefits reviews
When: held by the project sponsor during the benefits realisation phase — typically 6–12 months after project handover.
Benefits: check the project achieved the business benefits identified in the original business case; determine actions or improvements needed to support benefit realisation.
Audits
When: regularly held by personnel independent of the project, either at a set frequency or when an issue is identified.
Benefits: independent compliance assessment; increase stakeholder confidence; ensure the project follows the governance and processes defined in its quality management plans.
Exam tip: linear gates are event-driven at the end of each stage; iterative gates are time-bound at the end of each iteration. Benefits reviews: sponsor, 6–12 months after handover.
Detail on each review type
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not in the study pack.
Decision gates
Formal go / no-go points assessing entry and exit criteria; project may be paused, rescoped or terminated. Typically involve the sponsor, steering board and senior managers.
Life cycle placement
Gates after concept, definition and deployment; the post-project review around transition (scope, time, cost, quality, stakeholder satisfaction, lessons learned); benefit realisation reviews after handover to business as usual.
Benefits reviews
Use KPIs, benefit-tracking metrics and stakeholder feedback; remedial action if benefits fall short.
Audits
Check financial controls, procurement rules and quality standards; can be internal or external; can be planned or triggered by risks or anomalies.
Factors to report on
Reported as part of the review process to ensure successful project outcomes:
Progress against the schedule
Project spend — actual against planned
Earned value
Tracking project spend and understanding the value of the work achieved.
Audit findings and number of non-conformances
Risks and issues
Exam tip: five factors — schedule, spend (actual vs planned), earned value, audit findings and non-conformances, risks and issues. Learn the list.
Reporting detail
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not in the study pack.
Schedule
Shows slippage or acceleration, dependencies and bottlenecks; supports reallocating resources or compressing timelines.
Spend
Compares expenditure to the cost baseline; consistent gaps may signal poor planning, scope change or mismanagement.
Earned value
Combines cost, time and performance; indicators include CPI (cost performance index) and SPI (schedule performance index).
Risks and issues
A risk is a potential problem; an issue is an active event needing immediate attention. Repeated non-conformances may signal systemic weaknesses.
Decision making and information
For a review process to work, a broad ‘big picture’ view must be combined with in-depth analysis and detailed evidence, as required. Producing information and collating it as data is crucial for appropriate decision making and for communicating those decisions.
Six key uses of information and data
1. Status reporting
So the project manager can better understand the status of the project in relation to cost, schedule and quality against the acceptance criteria in the project management plan (PMP).
2. Estimating the project
So more realistic schedules and costs can be developed using past project data.
3. Risk identification
So the need for subsequent corrective actions, or the need for escalation, can be determined.
4. Problem solving and option appraisals
So options and solutions to project problems and issues can be generated using past project experience.
5. Availability of information
To inform decision making at key points, such as gate reviews, by ensuring relevant information is available in an appropriate format.
6. Knowledge and information management
To understand when best to schedule quality assurance and quality control activities, by assessing what was done on past projects and the optimum times in the life cycle to schedule them.
Exam tip: these six come straight from the study pack. Several use past project data (estimating, problem solving, knowledge management).
Information in practice
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not in the study pack.
  • Structured data collection reveals trends and emerging issues (e.g. completion data showing resource constraints; surveys showing disengagement)
  • Documenting decisions, actions and rationale gives traceability for governance reviews and audits
  • Tailored updates — dashboards, progress reports, executive summaries — keep stakeholders engaged and secure continued sponsorship
  • Information management: version control, access rights, confidentiality
Re-planning following reviews
After any review there may be a need to re-plan project activities to ensure overall success. This is aided by progress monitoring and thorough, meaningful reports.
Potential causes of re-planning
Scope
Achievement of planned scope doesn’t meet the requirement.
Team
Motivation and satisfaction of the project team is low.
Suppliers
Performance of contractors and / or relationships within the supply chain are poor.
Finance
Committed costs and cashflow exceed original forecasts.
Risk
Changes to the risk profile and / or impact on time and cost contingency are needed due to newly identified or changed risks.
Stakeholders
Communication with stakeholders is ineffective.
Exam tip: six causes — scope, team, suppliers, finance, risk profile, stakeholder communication. The sample paper asks for two.
Re-planning in context
Extra detail from the lecture narration — not in the study pack.
  • Re-planning is proactive governance, not an indication of failure
  • Quality problems may need extra quality assurance steps, revised acceptance criteria or rework
  • Supplier problems may need resequenced work packages, backup vendors or restructured procurement
  • Cost problems may need reallocated funds, rescoping or postponed deliverables

6.1 Why review throughout the life cycle (1 mark)
Answer B: to evaluate project performance.
6.2 Best demonstrates how a project is performing (1 mark)
Answer B: the actual costs versus forecast costs.
6.3 Primary purpose of reviews (1 mark)
Answer B: to gather information for decision making and communicate actions to stakeholders.
6.4 Which would NOT contribute to the need to re-plan? (1 mark)
Answer B: the governance structure. (New risks, lessons learned and approved scope changes can all drive re-planning.)
6.7 Earned value report; three key factors (2 marks)
(a) Earned value. (b) Quality, time and cost.
6.5 Decision gate: three questions, two benefits (5 marks)
Questions to answer ahead of a decision gate (1 mark each, 3 marks), e.g.:
  • What has been achieved?
  • What is required for the next stage?
  • What are the key decisions to be made?
  • Is the business case still viable?
Benefits of decision gates (1 mark each, 2 marks), e.g.:
  • Structured decision making
  • More effective risk management
  • Supports resource allocation
  • Ensures continued alignment with business objectives
  • Supports stakeholder engagement
  • Checks quality of deliverables so far
  • Checks the project is on track
6.6 Hardware migration scenario (5 marks)
Project behind schedule (specialists busy elsewhere, stakeholders unclear why it is urgent); budget underspent; no contingency used.
Two pieces of information to share (1 mark each), e.g.: progress against baseline schedule; budget vs spend so far; quality of deliverables; resource allocation; resource requirements; risk / issue log.
Three actions to get back on track (1 mark each), e.g.:
  • Spend some contingency to hire external specialists
  • Re-allocate internal specialists to support priorities
  • Communicate to stakeholders why the project is urgent, and help prioritise
  • Negotiate an extension with the existing hardware supplier
6.8 Two reasons to re-plan (2 marks)
  • New risks identified or existing risks changed
  • Project not performing: scope not delivered on schedule or deliverables below required quality
  • Team motivation or satisfaction is low
  • Stakeholder satisfaction low or communication poor
  • Poor supplier or contractor performance
  • Resource availability has changed
  • Budget off track: costs or cash flow above original forecasts
