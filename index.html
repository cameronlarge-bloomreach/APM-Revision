The framework through which decisions are made, accountability is upheld, and oversight is maintained throughout a project.

Sustainability in project management
Responsibilities, principles, and monitoring
What is sustainability in project management?
An approach to business that balances the environmental, social, and economic aspects of project-based working to meet the current needs of stakeholders without compromising or overburdening future generations.
The key principle
Building sustainability into the vision at project inception is key. If sustainability is planned in as a key requirement from the outset, it cannot then be taken out. It cannot be added retrospectively.
Exam tip: sustainability must be embedded at inception / initiation — not retrofitted at a later stage.
The three core principles
Balance
Address environmental, social, and economic needs without prioritising one at the expense of others.
Integration
Built into governance, processes, and culture — not treated as a separate or secondary track.
Foresight
Anticipate long-term impacts and design solutions that adapt to evolving expectations and regulations.
Sustainability across the lifecycle
Initiation
Include sustainability objectives in the business case and stakeholder analysis.
Planning
Assess resource selection, energy usage, and supply chain impact.
Execution
Decisions on materials, logistics, and workforce welfare.
Closure
How assets are decommissioned or repurposed affects the sustainability footprint.
The four dimensions of sustainability
Sustainability requires balancing all four — they are deeply interrelated and trade-offs between them are common.
Environmental
Climate change, biodiversity, energy efficiency. Reducing ecological footprint — carbon, waste, resource use.
Social
Human rights, equity and equality, community. Fairness, well-being, health & safety, labour practices.
Economic
Affordability, profit, risk management. Fair wages, ethical procurement, lifecycle costs.
Administrative
Legislation & regulation, health & safety, resource efficiency. Governance, policies, and standards.
Trade-offs
Trade-offs between dimensions are common and must be managed transparently. Example: recycled materials may increase costs (economic) but improve environmental outcomes. Use impact assessments, sustainability dashboards, and cost-benefit analysis to manage these transparently.
Sustainability vision and objectives
A sustainability vision defines overarching goals. Objectives translate aspirations into measurable targets. Involves both individual and organisational responsibility. The business case should be aligned to support the sustainability agenda.
Global frameworks to align with
UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
17 global goals providing a shared blueprint for peace and prosperity.
Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG)
Criteria used by investors to evaluate sustainability and ethical impact. Projects aligned with ESG gain investor confidence.
Paris Agreement
International treaty on climate change. High-emission projects must consider alignment with carbon reduction commitments.
What sustainability initiatives influence
  • Product design and manufacturing processes
  • Energy consumption and waste management
  • Procurement policies and supplier selection
  • Stakeholder relationships and community engagement
  • Performance metrics — measured alongside time, cost, and scope
Setting effective sustainability objectives
  • Realistic, measurable, and adaptable
  • Supported by clear KPIs, baselines, and reporting tools
  • Revisited throughout the lifecycle
  • Aligned with business strategy and operational practices
Why monitoring and reporting matter
Without monitoring and reporting, sustainability risks becoming an aspiration rather than an achievement. These mechanisms provide visibility, accountability, and insight.
Establishing baselines
Before any improvements can be measured, the current status must be understood. Baselines should cover: energy usage, waste output, carbon emissions, supplier practices, and employee well-being indicators. Aligned with organisational sustainability standards or external frameworks.
Embedding monitoring in governance
Sustainability metrics incorporated into regular status reports, dashboard reviews, risk logs, and decision points — reviewed by the project board with the same rigour as financial or schedule data.
Sustainability sits alongside time, cost, and quality in governance reporting — not as a peripheral concern.
Quantitative vs qualitative measurement
Quantitative
Emissions reduction, resource usage, compliance rates.
Qualitative
Stakeholder feedback, perception surveys, inclusivity and social impact assessment.
Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)
An international standard for sustainability reporting. Provides frameworks for data collection, data analysis techniques, and continuous improvement processes. Reports should include: progress against targets, explanations for variances, corrective actions, and plans for improvement.
Why stakeholder perception matters
Sustainability is not only about processes and performance — it is also about perception and trust. If stakeholders do not believe a project is genuinely committed to sustainability, they may withhold support, reduce collaboration, or escalate opposition.
Four stakeholder perception areas
Internal communities
Adjust culture internally to make sustainability part of day-to-day thinking. Must be "we want to do it" — not "we are made to do it".
External communities
Report on carbon emissions, energy consumption, waste management, social impact, and ethical practices. Messages should be tailored to different audiences.
Environmental, social & economic impacts
Transparency, credibility, and the importance of data and metrics are key. Aim for sustainable procurement.
Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)
Provides support for data collection, analysis techniques, and continuous improvement processes for sustainability practices.
Key phrases to know
  • "We want to do it" not "we are made to do it" — culture must be intrinsically motivated
  • Sustainability must move from aspiration to active behaviour through performance data
  • Transparent reporting on emissions, waste, and labour practices = proactive stance
  • Tailored communication for different audiences builds credibility
